# Sales & Pricing — Charging Real Money

Read when the founder is setting or raising a price, dodging a sales conversation, discounting by reflex, or walking into a negotiation. Underpricing is almost never a math problem — treat it as fear until proven otherwise.

## Value-based vs cost-plus

Cost-plus prices the founder's inputs. Value-based prices the customer's problem. The customer never sees the founder's costs and does not care; they see what the problem costs THEM — in money, time, pain, or status.

| Model | Question it answers | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Cost-plus | "What do I need to not lose money?" | Anchors at the floor; every efficiency gain gets donated to the customer |
| Competitor-match | "What is everyone else charging?" | Inherits competitors' fear; commoditizes the offer |
| Value-based | "What is this problem costing the buyer?" | Requires actually talking to buyers (see validation-playbook.md) |

Administer — the founder must answer, in order, before any price is set:
1. "What does this problem cost the buyer per month — in dollars, hours, or misery? Name a number or a concrete cost."
2. "What's the next-best alternative and its full price, including hassle?"
3. "What fraction of that value are you capturing at your current price?" Under ~10–20% capture is a signal to raise, not a virtue [inference — reasoning structure, verify against the founder's actual market].

The underpricing epidemic is fear wearing a spreadsheet costume. When a founder defends a low price with margin math, run the underpricing row of the block table in unblocking-protocols.md (mental contrasting): "What's the actual fear — losing the sale, or hearing no to your face?" Get the fear named out loud before touching the spreadsheet. Cost-plus still sets the FLOOR — price below landed cost + contribution target and the business is a charity (see finance-fundamentals.md for the margin math). The floor is not the price.

## Raise-price protocol

Administer in order. Do not let the founder skip step 1.

1. **Name the fear first.** Mental contrasting (unblocking-protocols.md). A price raise avoided for months is a psychology problem with a pricing costume.
2. **Test on new customers only.** New visitors have no anchor. Raise the public price; existing customers keep theirs for now. This isolates the demand signal from the loyalty signal.
3. **Set the kill-metric BEFORE launch.** "At what conversion drop does the raise lose money?" Rough structure: a price raise of X% tolerates a conversion drop of roughly X ÷ (X + gross margin %) before revenue falls — make the founder compute their own number with real margin [inference — arithmetic, verify with their figures]. Most founders have never run this and are shocked how much conversion they can afford to lose.
4. **Run it 2–4 weeks or to meaningful volume.** No peeking-and-panicking at day 3.
5. **Migrate existing customers deliberately.** Options: grandfather forever (loyalty asset, margin drag), grandfather 6–12 months with notice, or migrate with a plain-language email and an annual-prepay escape hatch. Never migrate silently.
6. **Measure churn against margin gained,** not against feelings. A few loud cancellations feel like catastrophe and are usually rounding error — demand the numbers.

Most founders discover demand was never about the price. When the raise holds, say so explicitly and log it — it is the strongest available evidence against the next round of underpricing fear.

## Price architecture

- **Anchoring** [peer-reviewed — Tversky & Kahneman, anchoring-and-adjustment]: the first number seen frames every later number. Show the premium option, the one-time price, or the problem's cost BEFORE the asking price. Never let the cheapest number lead the page or the call.
- **Good-better-best** [practitioner — standard tiering practice]: three tiers, the middle one engineered as the intended choice. The top tier exists partly to anchor; the bottom tier exists to catch price-sensitive buyers and make the middle look reasonable. One tier = a take-it-or-leave-it ultimatum; five+ = paralysis.
- **Decoy / asymmetric dominance** [peer-reviewed — Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982]: an option clearly worse than one target option (but not worse than the other) shifts choice toward the target. Ethical use: a decoy that clarifies real value differences. If the decoy only works by confusing the buyer, it's a dark pattern — veto it.
- **Subscription below one-time** (doctrine.md Law 5, AG1 pattern): price the subscription visibly below the one-time purchase so subscribing is the default rational choice. AG1's $79-vs-$99 gap drove ~75% subscription adoption. For any repeat-purchase product this is the highest-leverage architecture decision on the page.

## Discounting discipline

Every discount trains the customer. The question is what it trains them to do.

| Discount | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Launch/drop scarcity window | Strategy | Time-bound, reason-given, ends on schedule (pairs with doctrine.md Law 8) |
| Win-back for lapsed customers | Strategy | Targeted at revenue that was otherwise zero |
| First-subscription incentive | Strategy | Buys the habit; retention economics carry it (Law 5) |
| Habitual sitewide sales | Self-harm | Trains everyone to wait; full price becomes fiction |
| Panic discount to hit a weekly number | Self-harm | Sells margin to buy anxiety relief |
| Matching a competitor's race to the bottom | Self-harm | Their bad economics become the founder's |

Rule to administer: every discount must have a stated reason, a hard end date, and a one-line answer to "what does this train the customer to expect?" No answer, no discount. A founder discounting weekly is not marketing — run the underpricing intervention; same fear, different costume.

## Founder-led sales call structure

For any high-ticket, B2B, wholesale, or partnership conversation. Sequence is the discipline:

1. **Discovery before pitch.** Open with their situation, not the product. Fitzpatrick's Mom Test rules apply to sales discovery too (validation-playbook.md): past behavior and specifics, not hypothetical enthusiasm.
2. **Problem questions before product questions** [practitioner — Rackham's SPIN research on large-sale calls]: situation → problem → implication → payoff. Make the buyer articulate the cost of the problem; a buyer who states the cost has priced the solution for the founder.
3. **Pitch as a mirror.** Replay their own words: "You said X is costing you Y — here's how this addresses exactly that."
4. **State the price plainly, then stop talking.** One sentence, no cushioning, no "but we can work something out," no nervous laugh. The silence after the price belongs to the buyer. Founders who keep talking negotiate against themselves before the buyer says a word.

Pre-call: if stakes are high, run the Pressure Protocol (unblocking-protocols.md). Post-call debrief is feedforward, not autopsy — two things done differently next call.

## Negotiation basics

[Practitioner — Fisher & Ury's *Getting to Yes* (BATNA, interests vs positions); Voss's *Never Split the Difference* (tactical silence, calibrated questions).]

- **BATNA before the call** (Fisher & Ury). Make the founder write their actual walk-away alternative before entering. No BATNA = no leverage = every "no" feels existential. A weak BATNA is fixable (build the alternative first); an unknown BATNA is negligence.
- **Never negotiate against yourself.** One offer at a time. If the counterparty goes silent or says "you'll have to do better," the founder does not bid again — they ask what number the other side has in mind.
- **Trade concessions, never gift them.** Every give gets a get, stated in the same breath: "I can do X if you can do Y." Unreciprocated concessions teach the counterparty that pushing works — same training law as discounts.
- **Silence is a tool** (Voss). After a price, after a hard question, after their objection: count five slow beats. Discomfort with silence is where founders donate margin.
- **Interests over positions** (Fisher & Ury). When deadlocked on a number, ask what the number is FOR — payment timing, risk, volume, and term are all currencies that can break a price deadlock without touching the price.

## Handling "no"

Rejection is data; the founder's identity is not on the table. The mentor's job is keeping those two ledgers separate.

- After a "no," administer three questions: "What did they say verbatim?" (data, not the founder's dramatized summary). "Was it no-to-price, no-to-timing, no-to-fit, or no-to-you? Evidence?" (almost never the last one). "What changes on the next call?" — exactly two items, feedforward format (unblocking-protocols.md), zero autopsy.
- Watch for Colonna's fusion (unblocking-protocols.md): self-worth fused to the deal makes every no feel like identity threat — name the fusion when a routine rejection produces an outsized spiral.
- Volume reframe: a founder who cannot show a rejection count is not selling enough to have earned a conclusion about price. No pipeline data, no pricing verdict.
- Serial-no pattern (same objection ≥3 times) is signal, not noise: route to validation-playbook.md (offer problem) or marketing-acquisition.md (audience problem) before touching the price.

## Coaching hooks

- "What does this problem cost your customer per month? If you can't answer, you didn't price the product — you priced your fear."
- "Say the price out loud to me right now, then say nothing. If you can't do it here, you're not doing it on the call."
- "What did the last discount train your customers to do? Every discount is a training session — what's the lesson plan?"
- "What's your walk-away alternative if this deal dies? No BATNA, no negotiation — you're just receiving terms."
- "Was that a no to the price, or a no to you? Show me the verbatim quote — I'll bet it's not the one you're replaying."

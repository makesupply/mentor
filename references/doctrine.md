# Mentor Doctrine — The Laws

Condensed from Report 1 (founder case studies) and Report 2 (stage playbooks). Full sourced reports with citations: `references/research/`. When invoking a law with the founder, name the founder it came from and the number.

**Scope note — read before applying.** These laws were derived from a bootstrapped DTC-consumable/e-commerce cohort. The principles beneath them (focus before expansion, retention before acquisition scale, cash discipline, professionalize or plateau, crisis-as-curriculum) generalize to most businesses; the mechanisms (hero SKU, repeat rate, drops, inventory) are product-business-specific — translate, don't transplant, for SaaS/services/other models, and say you're translating. Law 6's bootstrap bias is a default posture with a stated exception, not a moral rule: a founder who deliberately chose venture funding for a winner-take-most market isn't violating doctrine — but they still owe the dilution math and the "what does this capital buy that revenue couldn't" answer.

**Law 1 — One hero SKU until retention is proven.** 9 of 12 documented founders scaled a single product through $10M+ (Gymshark fitted apparel, Ridge wallet, Native deodorant, Spanx, RXBAR, AG1's single SKU for a decade, Obvi collagen, MVMT watch, True Classic tee). Category expansion came AFTER retention economics were proven, as the $100M unlock — not before. Application: any new-product or new-venture impulse before the current product retains customers is a doctrine violation. Name it as one.

**Law 2 — Repeat rate before ad scale.** Native only scaled spend after fixing the melting-formula defect took repeat purchase from ~20% to 50%. Supplements have the leakiest first reorder of any consumable vertical (86.6% first-reorder rate — the lowest of the five wellness verticals Recharge measured). Application: ask for the second-order rate before blessing any spend increase.

**Law 3 — LTV:CAC ≥ 3:1, tracked personally, weekly.** Moiz Ali's hand-built ad spreadsheet ran a company that sold for $100M. Benchmarks: supplement CAC $53–78; 12-month subscriber LTV $300–600; below 2:1 is unprofitable. Application: at Stage 1 the founder owns this number personally. No delegation, no dashboard excuse.

**Law 4 — Never one channel.** Of the 50 dead brands in the DTC Graveyard 2026 report: 76% were single-channel, 94% had no loyalty program, and CAC rose 68% with nothing built to absorb it. Application: a second proven channel is a stage gate by $1M–$10M, not a nice-to-have.

**Law 5 — Retention infrastructure IS a growth lever.** AG1: subscription-default pricing ($79 subscription deliberately below $99 one-time → ~75% subscription adoption), eight-email onboarding, skip/swap/pause/win-back cancellation defense → LTV ~$711 vs ~$213 industry average. Annual billing holds ~96% net revenue retention at 12 months vs ~70% monthly. Supplements churn hardest at the FIRST reorder, then turn durable (deep-lifecycle skip rate 6.9% — skips, not cancels, keep subscribers alive). Application: a shipped retention feature usually beats an ad-spend increase at equal effort.

**Law 6 — Cash discipline; equity last.** Subscription prepay can push cash conversion cycle negative (-15 to -60 days) — customers fund the inventory. Ridge, Spanx, ButcherBox, Obvi, MVMT, and AG1 (12 years) reached $40M–$600M without meaningful outside equity; True Classic used revenue-based financing to survive its inventory crisis; Sean Frank uses debt facilities to preserve a decade-long horizon. Application: bootstrap bias; debt/RBF before dilution; equity is a last resort, not a milestone.

**Law 7 — Inventory by data, not gut.** True Classic's gut-feel over-order: "the stupidest decision we ever made" — 18 months digging out, $1M+ extra vendor interest. Ridge's hardest year (2018–19) was inventory plus a surprise tax bill. Application: the moment volume is real, demand planning becomes a documented process.

**Law 8 — Scarcity is a legitimate weapon.** Obvi deliberately under-stocked to manufacture FOMO; each restock roughly doubled the prior launch. Application: for a consumable with a community, drops and restocks beat always-in-stock at early stage — and reduce inventory risk (Law 7) at the same time.

**Law 9 — Creators before it's obvious; equity when possible.** Gymshark seeded 50–100k-subscriber fitness YouTubers "when nobody cared" and gave early influencers equity. Ridge has sponsored 5,000+ creators. AG1 pays Rogan (~$10M/yr) and Huberman (~$2M/yr) partly in equity — aligning incentives across years, not campaigns. Application: creator seeding is a Stage 1–2 lever a solo founder can run with AI leverage.

**Law 10 — Crisis is the curriculum.** Every fully documented founder had a near-death moment: lost manufacturer (Native), product defect (Native), inventory over-order (True Classic), inventory + tax (Ridge). The breakthrough founders converted each crisis into a system. Application: when a crisis hits, the mentor's question is "what system does this crisis build?"

**Law 11 — Professionalize or plateau.** Ben Francis handed the CEO seat to a Reebok veteran at 23 — revenue tripled — then took it back once he'd grown into it. AG1 hired Kat Cole. Native's 8-person team at $25–35M is the lean outlier proving how far founder+systems stretches — a solo founder's AI workforce can be their version of that leverage. Application: the founder's job migrates from doing the work to building the machine that does the work.

**Law 12 — What separates $100M-breakers from $10–30M stallers.** Three factors from the case evidence: (1) willingness to professionalize leadership, (2) retention economics prioritized over pure acquisition, (3) inventory/cash discipline institutionalized after crisis. And the psychology beneath it [peer-reviewed]: the differentiator is NOT risk appetite (findings are mixed to contradictory) — it is self-efficacy, internal locus of control, and boundary-protected consistency. Anxiety and loneliness measurably degrade decisions; mentor access correlates with ~50% higher resilience. That last number is this skill's job description.

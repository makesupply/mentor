# Coaching the Whole Founder: An Evidence Base for a Non-Clinical AI Mentor

## 1. Does Coaching Work? Effect Sizes and Mechanisms

Meta-analytic evidence establishes workplace/life coaching as a moderate-effect intervention, though effect sizes vary substantially by outcome type and study design. Theeboom, Beersma, and van Vianen's (2014) foundational meta-analysis of 18 studies found coaching produced significant positive effects across five individual-level outcomes: goal-directed self-regulation (Hedges' g = 0.74), work attitudes (g = 0.54), performance/skills (g = 0.60), well-being (g = 0.46), and coping (g = 0.43) [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. Notably, within-subjects (pre-post) designs yielded much larger effects (g = 1.15) than mixed designs with comparison groups (g = 0.39), signaling that some of the headline numbers are inflated by weaker methodology [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. The number of coaching sessions did not moderate outcomes — more sessions were not reliably better [peer-reviewed meta-analysis].[^1][^2][^3][^4]

Jones, Woods, and Guillaume's later meta-analysis (referenced widely as a follow-up to Theeboom) and a third synthesis converged on similar moderate effects, with one review concluding that "based on three meta-analyses representing thousands of data points, it is safe to conclude that coaching is an effective workplace intervention" [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. A more recent meta-analysis restricted to psychologically informed (CBT/positive-psychology-based) coaching found larger effects on goal attainment specifically (g = 1.29) and self-efficacy (g = 0.59), and larger effects on objectively-rated performance than self-report [peer-reviewed meta-analysis].[^2][^5]

### What Makes Coaching Work: The Working Alliance

Graßmann, Schölmerich, and Schermuly's (2020) meta-analysis of 27 samples (N = 3,563 coaching processes) found a moderate, consistent relationship between working alliance quality and coaching outcomes (r = .41, 95% CI [.34, .48]), with the strongest links to affective and cognitive outcomes and a negative association with unintended negative effects (r = −.29) [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. This held regardless of client type, coach expertise, or session count [peer-reviewed meta-analysis].[^6][^7]

However, this is a contested finding. A more recent trial-based reanalysis (De Haan et al., cited in VU Research Portal work) found that while a strong working alliance at the *start* of coaching predicted better outcomes, alliance strength did not correlate with *further* improvement across subsequent sessions — suggesting the alliance's causal role may be front-loaded rather than continuously operative [single study/reanalysis]. A separate two-wave survey of 181 Dutch coaching clients found working alliance explained only 2.3% of variance in goal attainment at follow-up — a real but "modest," not "moderate," effect, contradicting the meta-analytic estimate [single study]. Practically, this means an AI mentor's early rapport-building matters disproportionately, but alliance alone is not a sufficient engine for sustained behavior change.[^8][^9][^10]

## 2. Motivational Interviewing: Evidence Base and Adaptable Techniques

MI's evidence base is robust but the effect sizes are consistently small, and the technique-outcome link is more established than the "spirit"-outcome link. Lundahl et al.'s (2010) meta-analysis of 119 studies (842 effect sizes) found an average effect of g = 0.22 versus comparison conditions — small but statistically reliable [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. Judged against weak (waitlist/no-treatment) comparisons, MI produces a small but durable effect (g ≈ 0.28); judged against specific active treatments, the advantage nearly disappears (g ≈ 0.09) [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. A 2013 meta-analysis of MI in medical settings (48 studies, 9,618 participants) found OR = 1.55 (95% CI 1.40–1.71) — patients receiving MI were about 1.55 times more likely to improve [peer-reviewed meta-analysis].[^11][^12][^13][^14][^15]

Critically, a process-level meta-analysis testing MI's technical vs. relational hypotheses found that **MI-consistent techniques** (reflection, affirmation) — not general therapist empathy or "MI spirit" — predicted client change talk (r = .55) and downstream outcome improvements; the direct empathy-to-outcome and MI-spirit-to-outcome paths were *not* supported [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. This is directly transferable to non-clinical mentoring: the mechanism is not vibes, it's technique.[^16]

**Adaptable non-clinical techniques**, ranked by evidentiary support:
- Reflective listening / reflections — the strongest predictor of "change talk" (client language favoring change) [peer-reviewed meta-analysis][^16]
- Affirmations — statistically significant positive predictor of client satisfaction [single study, n-large][^11]
- Open questions that evoke the founder's own reasons for a goal, rather than persuasion — persuasion attempts are the weakest and sometimes counterproductive technique [peer-reviewed meta-analysis][^11][^16]
- Eliciting and reinforcing "change talk" specifically (not just any talk) — mediates the outcome effect [peer-reviewed meta-analysis][^16]

## 3. Values, Purpose, and Self-Concordance

### Self-Concordance Theory

Sheldon and Elliot's (1999) self-concordance model is a well-replicated framework, not a single study: goals pursued for autonomous reasons (intrinsic interest or identification with personal values) produce more sustained effort, higher attainment rates, and — critically — only autonomously-pursued goal attainment produces well-being gains, mediated by basic need satisfaction (autonomy, competence, relatedness) [peer-reviewed, replicated across >30 longitudinal studies]. Sheldon and Elliot (1999) itself fit three longitudinal datasets and reported a correlation of .29 between self-concordance and subjective well-being, replicated at r = .33 across four cultures in later work [peer-reviewed]. This is one of the more empirically secure constructs available for a mentor to draw on.[^17][^18][^19][^20]

A caveat for AI-mediated goal-setting specifically: a 2026 preregistered study found that when an AI conducted values-based goal-setting dialogue, the goal-progress benefit was mediated by *perceived social accountability* to the AI, not by increased self-concordance — self-concordance did not significantly mediate outcomes in that specific AI context [single study]. This suggests an AI mentor's value in values work may currently operate more through felt accountability than through actually shifting motivational structure — an important honesty point for design.[^21]

### Values Clarification: Evidence Is Mixed by Method

A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of 43 values clarification methods (33 RCTs) found explicit values clarification methods reduce values-incongruent choices (risk difference −0.04, 95% CI [−0.06, −0.02]) and decisional regret (SMD −0.20, 95% CI [−0.29, −0.11]) versus implicit methods or no clarification [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. Multicriteria decision analysis outperformed other clarification methods specifically [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. This literature originates in medical decision-aid research, not entrepreneurship coaching, so transfer to founder contexts is an inference, not a direct finding.[^22]

### Life-Domain Wheels: Honesty Check

**[SOURCE MISSING]** — No peer-reviewed psychometric validation was found in this research for the popular "Wheel of Life" tool used widely in coaching practice. It is a [practitioner] tool with face validity but no located reliability/validity data (e.g., no Cronbach's alpha, no test-retest data, no factor structure). By contrast, self-concordance measurement (Sheldon & Hoon's 4-item goal-motivation scale)  and the Klontz Money Script Inventory (below)  *do* have published psychometric validation. A mentor should administer the Wheel of Life (or similar domain-satisfaction check-ins) as a structured conversation prompt, not represent it as a validated instrument.[^19][^23][^24]

## 4. Relationships, Loneliness, and Social Support

### Entrepreneurship and Partnerships

Evidence on marriage/partnership strain is mixed and context-dependent. A 2026 dyadic structural-equation analysis of 628 UK couples (BHPS/UKHLS panel data) found entrepreneurial entry by one spouse lowers the other spouse's well-being through two channels: longer work hours (non-pecuniary) and reduced income (pecuniary); male founders transmit strain mainly via hours, solo start-ups mainly via income loss, employer start-ups mainly via time demands [peer-reviewed, large panel study]. Contrastingly, a Danish/CBS study of "entrepreneurial couples" who co-founded found no evidence of non-pecuniary costs or benefits to the relationship, and significant financial upside — especially for wives — that persisted even after firm dissolution [peer-reviewed]. A survey-based study of entrepreneurial families found 39.6% reported relationship strain from the business, over half felt some impact, but only ~14% of the divorced/separated/estranged subgroup attributed it clearly to the business, and goal congruity between spouses (not goal content itself) was the strongest predictor of spousal satisfaction [single study, moderate sample]. A frequently-cited popular claim ("15% more likely to divorce") traces to a specific study whose full citation could not be verified in this research [SOURCE MISSING — treat as practitioner claim, not confirmed meta-analytic finding].[^25][^26][^27][^28]

### Loneliness Interventions

This is one of the best-evidenced domains. Masi, Chen, Hawkley, and Cacioppo's (2011) seminal meta-analysis of 50 studies found interventions targeting maladaptive social cognition (reframing negative social expectations) produced the largest effects (mean ES up to −1.58 in some designs), outperforming social-support interventions (−0.34) and social-activity/access interventions (−0.27) [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. A larger 2024 APA-published meta-analysis (122 RCTs, n=13,971) found short-term effects of SMD = −0.50 (95% CI [−0.60, −0.39]) and long-term effects (33 multicohort studies) of SMD = −0.41 to −0.53 [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. A separate rapid review found psychological interventions had the largest effect (SMD −0.79) followed by social-interaction-based interventions (−0.50) and social-support-based interventions alone (−0.34) [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. The consistent message: cognitive reframing of social isolation outperforms simply "connecting more," which has direct implications for a founder-facing mentor — the intervention should target *maladaptive beliefs about being unsupported*, not just prompt more socializing.[^29][^30][^31][^32][^33]

### Social Support and Founder Performance

A study of 323 Chinese entrepreneurs found social support improves entrepreneurial well-being via two paths — directly, and indirectly by boosting entrepreneurial self-efficacy, which in turn improves entrepreneurial performance [single study, moderate sample, non-Western context]. This is a plausible mechanism but a single-study, single-culture finding, not meta-analytic.[^34]

## 5. Money Psychology

### Money Scripts (Klontz)

The Klontz Money Script Inventory (KMSI, 2011) and its revised form (KMSI-R, 2016, 32 items, Cronbach's alphas .70–.84) are empirically validated instruments identifying four belief patterns: money avoidance, money worship, money status, and money vigilance [peer-reviewed, validated instrument]. Money avoidance and money worship are associated with lower net worth, lower income, and higher revolving credit; money vigilance is associated with the wealthiest studied populations but also excessive anxiety [peer-reviewed, large sample "tens of thousands"]. Klontz's research links specific scripts to disordered behaviors including workaholism, financial enabling, and financial enmeshment — several of which are directly relevant to founder psychology [peer-reviewed]. This is a genuinely conversational, non-clinical-feeling instrument a mentor could administer via structured questions.[^35][^23][^36][^24]

### Scarcity Mindset: Contested Replication Status

Mullainathan and Shafir's scarcity/"cognitive bandwidth" theory proposes that financial scarcity taxes attention and executive function, causing tunneling and over-borrowing [peer-reviewed, original theory]. Replication status is genuinely mixed — this must be reported honestly. Shah, Mullainathan, and Shafir's own 2019 self-replication of their 2012 paper found 9 of 13 original analyses replicated, 2 were directionally consistent but much weaker, and 2 did not replicate; the over-borrowing effect was robust, but the "cognitive fatigue from scarcity-induced focus" hypothesis was not supported in replication [peer-reviewed self-replication]. A separate replication of the related Mani et al. (2013) "poverty taxes bandwidth" study, run on 417 Colombian high-school students, found *no* evidence that scarcity taxed cognitive or executive function, contradicting the original [peer-reviewed replication, different population]. The core over-borrowing/attentional-focus effect appears more robust than the "cognitive impairment" claim, which should be treated as contested, not settled science [peer-reviewed, mixed evidence].[^37][^38][^39][^40]

## 6. The Physical Foundation

### Sleep

A 2024 preregistered meta-analysis (154 studies, 1,338 effect sizes, N=5,717) found all forms of sleep loss reduced positive affect (SMD −0.27 to −1.14), increased anxiety symptoms (SMD 0.57–0.63), and blunted emotional arousal responses (SMD −0.20 to −0.53) [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. A separate meta-analysis of sleep deprivation's effect on inhibitory control (a core executive function for decision-making) across 24 papers/30 effect sizes found a moderate negative effect (Hedges' g = −0.475, 95% CI [−0.604, −0.346]) on commission errors in Go/No-Go tasks [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. This gives concrete, quantified grounding for "sleep affects your judgment" advice.[^41][^42]

### Exercise

Exercise's cognitive benefits are real but the size is smaller than popular claims suggest, and publication bias meaningfully deflates the "true" effect. A large 2025 umbrella review (107 meta-analyses, ~82,742 participants) found exercise improved general cognition (SMD=0.42), memory (SMD=0.26), and executive function (SMD=0.24) — small-to-moderate effects — but a separate Bayesian meta-meta-analysis of 215 meta-analyses (2,239 effect sizes) that specifically adjusted for selective reporting found dramatically smaller "true" effects: general cognition SMD=0.227, memory SMD=0.027 (not clearly different from zero), executive function SMD=0.012 (essentially null) [peer-reviewed meta-analysis, publication-bias-corrected]. Acute (single-session) exercise shows a small, more reliable boost to executive function/reaction time (g≈0.13–0.36 depending on analysis) [peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. The honest summary: exercise modestly helps mood and short-term executive function; broad claims of "generalized cognitive enhancement" are premature and likely inflated by publication bias [peer-reviewed, contested].[^43][^44][^45][^46][^47]

### Stress Physiology

Acute stress is well-documented to impair working memory and cognitive control according to major meta-analytic syntheses, though the specific studies retrieved here were secondary citations rather than primary meta-analyses [inference from cited literature].[^48]

## 7. Identity, Transitions, and Growth

### Identity Fusion and Business Failure

A qualitative study of 16 entrepreneurs whose businesses failed identified two distinct identity-reconstruction patterns following failure: some entrepreneurs recovered relatively fast by drawing on prior work identities to rebuild ("looking backward to move forward"), while others underwent a more complex, prolonged struggle to let go of who they were before constructing a new identity [peer-reviewed, qualitative, small sample]. This is a single qualitative study — informative about mechanism, not generalizable in magnitude.[^49]

### Athlete Retirement as an Analog

The athlete-retirement literature is a mature, useful analog for founder identity work because "high athletic identity" (identity fusion with a singular role) is consistently associated with worse retirement adjustment across 23 reviewed academic reviews [peer-reviewed scoping review]. A prospective longitudinal study found that athletes who proactively diminished the prominence of their athletic identity *before* retirement avoided major identity crises, supporting a "diversify identity before the transition" strategy directly transferable to founders [peer-reviewed, prospective, small n=6]. A larger mixed-methods study (n=138 retiring elite athletes) found athletic identity dropped 32% (d=1.2) post-retirement with a U-shaped mental-health trajectory peaking at 3 months, and multimodal interventions combining CBT, narrative identity work, and lifestyle redesign produced 41% lower depression scores and faster physiological (cortisol) recovery versus standard transition support [peer-reviewed, single trial]. The Social Identity Model of Identity Change offers a validated theoretical frame: identity loss is easier when the person maintains multiple, non-exclusive identities pre-transition [peer-reviewed, qualitative n=21].[^50][^51][^52][^53][^54]

### Post-Traumatic Growth

Tedeschi and Calhoun's (1995/1996) model, operationalized via the 21-item Post-Traumatic Growth Inventory, identifies five empirically-derived growth domains: relating to others, new possibilities, personal strength, spiritual/existential change, and appreciation of life [peer-reviewed, validated instrument, 30 years of research]. Critically, growth requires *deliberate* rumination (voluntary, meaning-focused reflection) following an initial period of intrusive rumination — a person stuck in intrusive rumination trends toward chronic distress instead [peer-reviewed model]. Roughly half to two-thirds of people studied show measurable PTG after major adversity, and openness to experience and extraversion predict higher likelihood of growth [peer-reviewed, APA summary]. This gives a mentor concrete language for reframing business failure or major setbacks: not "get over it" but "move from intrusive to deliberate processing."[^55][^56][^57][^58]

## 8. Where Coaching Must Stop

The International Coaching Federation's Code of Ethics (2020/2025) explicitly requires coaches to "recognize the boundaries of coaching" and refer clients when needs fall outside coaching competence, including signs of clinical depression, anxiety, trauma, acute emotional distress, or any issue requiring diagnosis or clinical treatment [professional/practitioner ethical standard]. Suicidal ideation or threats of harm require immediate referral and may require breaking confidentiality [professional standard].[^59][^60]

For screening, validated brief clinical instruments exist and are well-established in primary care and digital-health contexts, though they are diagnostic/clinical tools, not coaching tools — a mentor should use them only as referral triggers, never as diagnosis: PHQ-9 (9-item depression severity) and its ultra-brief version PHQ-2, and GAD-7 (7-item generalized anxiety) with brief version GAD-2, and the combined PHQ-4 [peer-reviewed, widely validated clinical instruments]. These are cited extensively across clinical AI and digital mental-health literature as the standard first-line screening layer before any therapeutic engagement [peer-reviewed].[^61][^62][^63][^64]

**[SOURCE MISSING]**: No systematic evidence in this research quantifies the outcome rate of coach-to-clinician referrals (i.e., "how often does appropriate referral actually improve outcomes") — this is a gap in the evidence base itself, not just a gap in this search. The referral obligation is an ethical/professional-standard claim, not an outcomes-validated one.

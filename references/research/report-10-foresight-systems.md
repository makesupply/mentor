# Foresight and Failure-Prevention Systems: Evidence Base for an AI Mentor's "Foresight Mode"

## Overview

Foresight systems combine two complementary movements: looking forward to imagine failure before it happens (premortems, reference-class forecasting, scenario planning) and looking backward to extract lessons after outcomes are known (after-action reviews, blameless postmortems). The evidence base for these practices ranges from a well-replicated laboratory finding with a precise effect size, to widely-adopted-but-thinly-evaluated management rituals, to techniques with documented failures to replicate. This report separates "proven to improve forecasts/outcomes" from "widely used but unevaluated," using [peer-reviewed], [military-institutional doctrine], and [practitioner] labels throughout, and closes with a ranked, evidence-weighted design for a solo-founder foresight system.

## The Premortem and Prospective Hindsight

The premortem's psychological foundation is a 1989 experiment by Deborah Mitchell (Wharton), J. Edward Russo (Cornell), and Nancy Pennington (University of Colorado), published as "Back to the Future: Temporal Perspective in the Explanation of Events" in the *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making* [peer-reviewed]. The study compared two framings of a future event: a "prospective" group told the event *might* happen and asked to list reasons, versus a "prospective hindsight" group told the event *had already happened* and asked to explain why. The hindsight framing produced measurably more — and more concrete, action-based — explanations than the prospective framing. The commonly cited headline figure is that prospective hindsight increases the ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes by roughly 30%, with certainty (not temporal direction) driving the effect, and certainty adding about twice as many action-based versus abstract reasons. One important caveat worth flagging: a corrective analysis argues the original 1989 result specifically measured the *number* of reasons generated, not the *accuracy* of those reasons — a distinction that later popularizations sometimes blur [peer-reviewed].

Gary Klein, a cognitive psychologist known for naturalistic decision-making research, formalized this finding into the "premortem" method, first developing it around 1989–1992 and publishing the definitive version as "Performing a Project Premortem" in *Harvard Business Review* in September 2007. Klein's procedure is mechanical and runs 20–30 minutes:

- The facilitator announces that the plan has already failed catastrophically — not partially, completely.
- Each participant spends 2–10 minutes independently and silently writing every reason they can think of for the failure, crucially before any discussion (to prevent anchoring on the most senior voice).
- The group goes round-robin, each person reading one reason per round until the list is exhausted, recorded without judgment.
- The facilitator leads a brief discussion identifying which failure modes are most probable, most severe, and already visible in the current plan.
- The project leader revises the plan to address the highest-probability, highest-severity failure modes.

Klein himself frames the premortem as "the hypothetical opposite of a postmortem" — coming at the beginning of a project rather than the end, so problems can be designed out rather than autopsied afterward. Documented organizational use spans NASA, Google, the US Army, and McKinsey engagements, and McKinsey explicitly recommends it as a debiasing tool for major capital and strategy decisions. A key mechanistic advantage over standard "what could go wrong?" brainstorming is social permission: premortems make it psychologically safe to voice reservations that would otherwise seem disloyal or overly pessimistic during planning, directly counteracting overinvestment and groupthink.

## The Outside View: Reference-Class Forecasting and the Planning Fallacy

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky documented in 1979 that people forecasting a project focus on its specific features and construct an optimistic completion scenario while ignoring the base rate — the historical distribution of outcomes for similar past efforts [peer-reviewed]. Kahneman and Dan Lovallo named this the "planning fallacy" in 2003 and traced it to an "inside view" that focuses on the constituents of the specific plan rather than outcomes of similar completed plans. The prescribed corrective is the "outside view": basing forecasts on a reference class of comparable past cases rather than the specifics of the case at hand.

Bent Flyvbjerg operationalized this into reference-class forecasting (RCF), a three-step method: (1) identify a reference class of past, comparable projects broad enough to be statistically meaningful but narrow enough to be truly comparable; (2) establish an empirical probability distribution of outcomes for that class; (3) position the current project within that distribution and adjust only where hard evidence justifies a departure [peer-reviewed]. Kahneman later called this "the single most important piece of advice regarding how to increase accuracy in forecasting."

The results in practice are substantial and sobering. Flyvbjerg's database of infrastructure megaprojects found nine out of ten rail projects overestimate passenger demand (average overestimation 106%), and for half of all road projects actual traffic differs from forecast by more than ±20% — with no improvement in forecast accuracy over a 30-year study period [peer-reviewed]. Only about 8.5% of megaprojects meet both cost and time targets, and only 0.5% meet cost, time, and benefit targets combined. Category-specific mean cost overruns range from roughly +1% for solar power up to +238% for nuclear waste storage and +157% for Olympic Games. Where RCF has actually been applied — Hong Kong roadworks projects and UK/Dutch transport appraisal — cost-overrun frequency and magnitude measurably declined relative to non-RCF benchmarks, and the American Planning Association formally endorsed the method in 2005 [peer-reviewed]. Laboratory replications by Buehler, Griffin, and colleagues corroborate that anchoring on historical base rates produces meaningfully better accuracy than inside-view estimation alone [peer-reviewed]. The mechanism connecting this to base-rate neglect is direct: vivid, case-specific details crowd out attention to the prior probability that should anchor any single estimate.

## Forecasting Skill: The Good Judgment Project and Superforecasters

Philip Tetlock's Good Judgment Project (2011–2015, part of the IARPA ACE tournament) identified that the top ~2% of roughly 25,000 forecasters — "superforecasters" — sustained Brier scores (a proper scoring rule where 0 is perfect and 0.25 is what constant 50/50 guessing yields) around 0.14–0.16 on structured, long-horizon geopolitical questions, compared to roughly 0.19–0.37 for ordinary forecasters and intelligence-community analysts, a persistent 30%+ advantage that compounded to over 60% by the tournament's fourth year [peer-reviewed]. Tetlock's account (with Dan Gardner) attributes this to specific, learnable practices rather than innate gift: converting vague questions into precise, falsifiable ones; anchoring on base rates before layering in case-specific context; decomposing complex questions into tractable sub-questions (Fermi-style estimation); balancing inside and outside views; making frequent, small, granular probability updates (Bayesian updating in small increments rather than large swings); and actively seeking disconfirming evidence. Barbara Mellers's analysis found that granularity itself predicts accuracy — superforecasters using probability increments as fine as 1% lost meaningful accuracy when their forecasts were artificially rounded to the nearest 5%, while ordinary forecasters lost little accuracy even when rounded to the nearest 20% — indicating genuine, not illusory, precision [peer-reviewed].

Whether this transfers to individuals and how much training alone helps is measurable and only partially favorable. One benchmark study found: baseline Brier score 0.21; brief calibration/probabilistic-reasoning **training alone** improves it modestly to 0.19; putting trained individuals into **teams** that question each other's forecasts drops it further to 0.14 (driven mostly by noise reduction); and teams of superforecasters reach as low as 0.08 [peer-reviewed]. This implies training moves the needle only slightly on its own — the larger gains come from structured team deliberation and from the sustained, scored practice that identifies genuine skill in the first place, which is a harder thing for a solo founder to replicate. Good Judgment's own commercial curriculum — probabilistic reasoning, base rates, inside/outside views, over/underconfidence, calibration — is explicitly designed as a transferable skill-training product, evidence that the constituent techniques (not necessarily elite-level accuracy) are teachable to non-experts [practitioner]. For probability elicitation for non-experts specifically, the actionable takeaways are: force numeric probabilities rather than verbal hedges ("likely," "possible"), use fine-grained percentage points rather than coarse buckets, and require an explicit base rate before any case-specific adjustment.

## Scenario Planning: The Shell Tradition and Thin Evidence on Decision Quality

Scenario planning originated at Royal Dutch Shell, tracing to 1966–1971 work in its Exploration & Production function assessing political risk, formalized by Pierre Wack in the early 1970s. Wack's "Rapids" scenarios in 1972 are credited with helping Shell foresee the 1973–74 oil price shock, though this claim has been characterized as partly mythologized by Wack's own retrospective framing and by his disciple Peter Schwartz, who later brought the method to the Global Business Network. Van der Heijden later formalized the methodology (horizon scanning of influential factors, ranking by impact/uncertainty, building extreme scenarios around the two key uncertainties, articulating implications).

Being honest about the evidence: rigorous, generalizable studies showing that scenario planning *causally improves* decision quality or organizational performance are scarce. Available evidence is largely case-study and practitioner-testimonial in nature (Shell's own retrospective narrative, consulting-firm case chapters describing single-firm applications to bankruptcy/reinvention exercises) rather than controlled comparisons. One preliminary academic evaluation of a structured scenario-planning protocol found a batch success rate of 87.5% (7/8 cases) and a blind evaluation preferring protocol output over unstructured brainstorming (74/80 vs 49/80), but the authors explicitly caveat this as "proof-of-concept" evidence, executed by a single operator with AI assistance — not a validated, generalizable result [peer-reviewed, self-described as preliminary]. ## Risk Management Scaled Down

### Risk registers and likelihood × impact scoring

Risk registers and the likelihood-times-impact matrix are the most widely used risk-prioritization tool in project and enterprise risk management, but the technique has well-documented mathematical flaws. Tony Cox's 2008 *Risk Analysis* paper "What's Wrong with Risk Matrices?" identified four core problems [peer-reviewed]:

| Flaw | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Poor resolution | Typical matrices correctly and unambiguously rank fewer than 10% of randomly selected hazard pairs; very different risks get identical ratings ("range compression") |
| Ranking errors | Matrices can assign a *higher* qualitative rating to a *quantitatively smaller* risk, especially when frequency and severity are negatively correlated — in these cases the matrix can be "worse than useless," producing worse-than-random decisions |
| Suboptimal resource allocation | Categorical outputs cannot support genuinely optimal allocation of mitigation resources |
| Ambiguous inputs/outputs | Verbal likelihood/severity categories ("rare," "possible," "likely") are ordinal, not interval, data; multiplying them (e.g., 3×4=12) implies false numerical precision, and different users interpret the same category differently |

Douglas Hubbard has separately argued matrices create an "analysis placebo effect" — the ritual feels rigorous but performs no better than chance in some tested configurations. This does not mean risk registers are useless — they remain valuable for surfacing and tracking risks and facilitating discussion — but the numeric score they produce should not be treated as a precise, additive metric, and correlated risks (shared root causes across multiple listed items) are typically ignored by the matrix format entirely.

### Tripwires and decision points

The corrective that both Klein's decision research and military doctrine converge on is pre-committing to specific, observable trigger conditions rather than relying on periodic subjective re-assessment. US Army doctrine (FM 101-5) formally defines a "Decision Point" as an event or location where a tactical decision is required during mission execution — decision points do not dictate *what* the decision is, only that one must be made, tied to pre-identified critical events and named/target areas of interest, established through a deliberate "war-gaming" process before execution begins [military-institutional doctrine]. One of three DP types is explicitly a "trigger": a pre-set point in time or space established in advance to initiate an action once crossed. The broader Observe-Orient-Decide-Act (OODA) loop, developed by USAF Colonel John Boyd, frames tripwires as accelerants of the observe/orient phases — the side that cycles through the loop faster gains decision advantage [military-institutional doctrine]. Applied to a small business, this converts "monitor cash flow" (vague, ritual) into "if cash runway drops below six weeks, trigger the pre-agreed contingency plan" (a tripwire) — precisely the kind of falsifiable, pre-committed condition superforecasters use to force honest probability estimates. Premortem practitioner guides explicitly recommend converting the exercise's failure narratives into exactly these observable tripwires and invalidation conditions.

### Leading vs. lagging indicators for small businesses

Lagging indicators (revenue, profit, churn after the fact) confirm damage has already occurred; leading indicators (weekly cash runway, pipeline conversion rate, customer concentration percentage, days sales outstanding, ad account health/CPM trend) are the signals a tripwire should actually watch, because they move before the lagging outcome does. ## High-Reliability Learning Loops

### After Action Review (Army) and debriefing meta-analysis

The US Army's After Action Review (AAR) is a structured, collaborative post-training/post-mission discussion designed for self-learning and self-correction by the team involved, rather than top-down critique [military-institutional doctrine]. A meta-analysis synthesizing 61 studies (107 effect sizes, 915 teams, 3,499 individuals) found debriefs/AARs produce an overall improvement of d = 0.79 in training-evaluation criteria — a substantial effect, larger than many other training-method effects in the literature [peer-reviewed]. Two features consistently drove effectiveness: alignment of the review to the individual or team being assessed, and use of objective performance-review media (data/recordings) rather than pure recollection. Structure helps more in military contexts than in healthcare contexts, where highly and lightly structured AARs performed comparably. AAR effectiveness overall depends on discussion participation, focus on specific learning points, timeliness (short delay before the AAR), and reinforcement afterward [military-institutional doctrine].

### Checklist evidence, including where it failed to replicate

Peter Pronovost's five-item central-line insertion checklist (hand hygiene, full barrier precautions, chlorhexidine skin cleaning, avoiding the femoral site, removing unnecessary catheters), paired with a cultural change empowering nurses to halt a procedure if a step was skipped, was deployed across the Michigan Keystone ICU Project (103–121 ICUs) [peer-reviewed]. Results: median catheter-related bloodstream infection rate fell from 2.7 per 1,000 catheter-days at baseline to 0 within three months (P≤0.002), a 66% reduction sustained at 16–18 months, an estimated 1,500 lives and $175 million saved in the first 18 months, and effects sustained at 4, 5, and 10 years [peer-reviewed]. A follow-up Medicare-claims analysis found a 10% reduction in ICU patient mortality associated with the program, one of very few quality-improvement initiatives to show a mortality effect at this scale [peer-reviewed].

Atul Gawande's WHO Surgical Safety Checklist pilot (eight hospitals, 2007–08) reported complications falling by more than a third and deaths by almost half — but this was a before/after design without randomization or a control group, a limitation Gawande himself acknowledges. Crucially, later replication attempts diverged: an analysis of over 200,000 procedures across 101 Ontario hospitals found **no significant reduction** in complications or deaths after checklist introduction; a study modeled on Pronovost's checklist found no improvement at some Michigan hospitals; and a UK-wide replication of the Keystone program ("Matching Michigan") found **no difference** in infection rates. The consensus explanation is that checklists work when paired with genuine behavioral/cultural change (authority to stop, team buy-in, measurement feedback) and fail when implemented as a paperwork formality — the checklist is necessary but not sufficient. This is the single clearest documented case in this report of a foresight/reliability tool that is evidence-backed in its origin study but has failed to replicate under less careful implementation — a caution directly relevant to any "checklist" component of an AI mentor.

### Blameless postmortems (practitioner)

John Allspaw, then CTO of Etsy, formalized the "blameless postmortem" in a widely cited 2012 post, building on the "Just Culture" concept from aviation and medicine safety science [practitioner]. The core moves: treat causes as "constructed, not found" (the accident model chosen determines what gets blamed); apply "local rationality" — ask how the action made sense to the person at the time, given the information they actually had, rather than judging with hindsight; shift from "First Stories" (human error as cause) to "Second Stories" (human error as a symptom of systemic vulnerabilities); and deliberately avoid repeated "why" questions that converge on "who," replacing them with "how" questions that surface systemic conditions [practitioner]. This is a mature, widely adopted practitioner methodology in software engineering (Google SRE also documents a blameless postmortem culture) but its causal effect on incident rates has not been isolated in controlled studies — it is best classified as a well-reasoned, widely adopted [practitioner] practice rather than a [peer-reviewed] one.

## What Actually Goes Wrong in 30/60/90-Day Windows

CB Insights' post-mortem analysis of failed startups (483 cases) found the most cited causes were: running out of cash or failing to raise (38%), no market need (35%), being outcompeted (20%), a flawed business model (19%), regulatory/legal challenges (18%), pricing/cost issues (15%), wrong team (14%), mistimed product (10%), poor product (8%), and team/investor disharmony (7%) — with most failures citing two or more causes simultaneously, i.e., failure is rarely a single-point event [peer-reviewed/industry]. A separate, oft-cited U.S. Bank small-business study found roughly 82% of small-business failures had cash flow as a contributing factor — a broader claim than the narrower "ran out of cash as primary cause" statistic, and the two figures should not be conflated. CB Insights also found failed startups typically die about 20 months after their last financing round, after having raised roughly $1.3 million — i.e., cash-out is a lagging, cliff-edge event that is frequently visible well in advance through burn-rate and runway tracking, which is precisely the kind of leading indicator a rolling foresight window should be tracking.

Mapped onto a 30/60/90-day cadence relevant to a solo e-commerce or CPG operator, the recurring short-horizon precipitants implied by this literature are:

- Cash gaps and payment timing — the dominant startup-failure precipitant, and the one most detectable early via runway and receivables tracking.
- Platform or algorithm changes — not directly quantified in the retrieved literature but structurally analogous to the "external pressures" category CB Insights cites in 29% of shutdowns (bad timing, unfavorable macro/market shifts). [SOURCE MISSING: no dataset specifically quantifying ecommerce/advertising-platform algorithm-change impact on small-business revenue was located in this pass.]
- Customer concentration events and key-person events — echoed in the "team issues" (23%) and "disharmony" (7%) categories, though these studies focus on venture-backed startups rather than solo-founder SMBs specifically.
- Supply disruptions and seasonal traps — not directly evidenced in the retrieved sources for this report; this should be flagged as [SOURCE MISSING] rather than asserted with a specific figure.

## Cadence: How Often to Review, and When Premortems Become Ritual

The most concrete cadence evidence comes from OKR-adjacent goal-tracking research rather than premortem-specific studies. Across a 2026 benchmark dataset of 200 organizations and a separate platform dataset of 876 organizations (7,419 objectives, 20,952 key results), teams reviewing goals weekly completed 43% more of their objectives than teams reviewing monthly or ad hoc, and teams skipping the weekly rhythm were three times more likely to abandon the goal-tracking system altogether [industry benchmark, not peer-reviewed]. Critically, longer reviews performed worse: completion peaked at 15–20 minutes per check-in, while sessions of 45+ minutes underperformed shorter ones — the value came from cadence and a short, forcing set of questions (what moved, what's blocked, what's next, who owns it), not from meeting length or depth. A separate survey of OKR practice found top-performing "overachiever" organizations were 4.7 times more likely than beginners to run weekly or monthly check-ins rather than quarterly-only reviews.

Extrapolating cautiously from this (goal-cadence, not premortem-specific) evidence and the decision-point/tripwire literature: a rolling 30/60/90-day premortem should be refreshed on a matching rolling basis — light weekly tripwire checks (5–15 minutes, mechanical: has any pre-set trigger fired?), a fuller monthly premortem-refresh (are the top failure scenarios still the right ones, given what changed this month?), and a deeper quarterly reset (re-run the full premortem exercise from scratch, since a 90-day-old failure narrative is likely stale). [SOURCE MISSING: no study was found directly measuring at what refresh frequency premortems specifically become ritualized and lose signal value; the risk of ritualization is widely asserted in practitioner writing on premortems and risk registers but not quantified.] The general principle that emerges across both the checklist-replication-failure evidence and the risk-matrix critique is consistent: the ritual's value decays whenever it stops being tied to a live, consequential decision and starts being performed for its own sake.

## Synthesis: A Minimal Viable Foresight System for a Solo Founder

Ranked by strength of evidence, from strongest to weakest:

1. **Premortem with independent silent writing before discussion** — the strongest single-technique evidence in this report: a specific, replicated 30% effect size from a peer-reviewed 1989 study, operationalized into a documented, widely adopted method used by NASA, Google, and the US Army. For a solo founder, this becomes a solitary exercise: assume the 90-day plan failed, write every reason before rationalizing any of them.
2. **Tripwires converted from premortem failure modes into specific observable numbers** — grounded in military decision-point doctrine and directly recommended by premortem practitioners as the exercise's output, avoiding the well-documented mathematical flaws of likelihood×impact scoring.
3. **Reference-class check on any specific numeric forecast** (revenue, timeline, ad spend payback) — peer-reviewed, with measured real-world accuracy gains where applied, though it requires a genuine comparable dataset to work, which is often scarce for a small, idiosyncratic business.
4. **Short, high-frequency check-ins on the tripwires (weekly, 15–20 min) with a deeper monthly/quarterly premortem refresh** — supported by large-N (though industry, not peer-reviewed) goal-cadence data showing a 43% completion lift and a strong penalty for long meetings.
5. **Blameless postmortem after any tripwire fires or plan misses**, using "how did this make sense at the time" rather than "why did you do this" — mature practitioner methodology, not independently outcome-validated, but low-cost and directly complementary to the premortem loop.
6. **A lightweight, single-page scenario sketch (not a full Shell-style six-step exercise)** — retained for its plausible mechanism (forcing more than one future into view) but ranked last because the evidence that scenario planning itself improves decisions is thin and mostly case-study-based rather than causally demonstrated.

The weakest-evidence components to deliberately avoid over-engineering are a numeric risk-matrix score (mathematically unsound per Cox 2008) and a checklist implemented as pure documentation without genuine authority to halt or escalate when a step is skipped — the exact condition under which checklist replications have failed.

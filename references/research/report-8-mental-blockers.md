# The Founder's Mind: A Psychological Blocker Map with Evidence-Graded Coaching Interventions

Entrepreneurial failure to execute, grow, or learn is rarely a single defect — it is usually a cluster of overlapping cognitive-emotional patterns that compound one another. This report maps ten empirically studied psychological blockers, grades the evidence behind each, and specifies what a non-clinical AI mentor can and cannot do about them.

## 1. Procrastination

**Construct and research base.** Piers Steel's 2007 meta-analysis (691 correlations) frames procrastination as "the voluntary delay of an intended course of action despite expecting to be worse off for the delay," driven by low expectancy (self-efficacy), low task value, high impulsiveness, and delay-sensitivity — formalized in Temporal Motivation Theory (TMT) as Motivation = (Expectancy × Value) / (1 + Impulsiveness × Delay) [peer-reviewed]. Steel found procrastination's largest correlates were trait impulsiveness and low conscientiousness (r ≈ 0.3–0.4) [peer-reviewed]. Sirois and Pychyl's competing (complementary, not contradictory) emotion-regulation model reframes procrastination as short-term mood repair: an aversive task generates negative affect, avoidance provides immediate relief, and "we give in to feel good" (Tice & Bratslavsky's phrase) — with shame and guilt from the delay itself feeding the next avoidance cycle [peer-reviewed].[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5]

**Prevalence/severity in founders.** Direct founder-specific prevalence data is not well established in the reviewed literature; procrastination research is dominated by student and general-adult samples. [SOURCE MISSING] for founder-specific base rates.

**Interventions.** A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (646 participants) found procrastination-reduction interventions produce a large effect, Hedges' g = 1.18, 95% CI [0.71, 1.65] [peer-reviewed]. Effective, coaching-adaptable components include: implementation intentions ("if it's 9:00, then I open the draft"), task chunking to reduce the aversiveness threshold, and self-forgiveness/emotion-tolerance skills training, which Sirois's 2023 review links to reduced repeat procrastination [peer-reviewed]. Academic-context studies also support micro-goal decomposition (Ferrari; Pychyl) [practitioner].[^6][^5][^7]

**Founder language markers.** "I'll start once I have the perfect plan," "I keep opening the doc and closing it," "I know I should be doing outreach but I clean my inbox instead."

## 2. Perfectionism

**Construct and research base.** Hewitt and Flett's tripartite model (1991) distinguishes self-oriented perfectionism (self-imposed standards), other-oriented perfectionism (demanding others be perfect), and socially prescribed perfectionism (perceiving others demand perfection of you) [peer-reviewed]. Socially prescribed perfectionism is the subtype most robustly linked to neuroticism and psychological maladjustment [peer-reviewed].[^8][^9][^10]

**Perfectionism-procrastination link (contested).** Steel's 2007 meta-analysis found virtually no relationship (r = −0.03) when perfectionism was treated as unidimensional — leading to his famous claim that "procrastinators are actually less likely, not more, to be perfectionists" [peer-reviewed]. Sirois, Molnar, and Hirsch's 2017 meta-analytic update, splitting the construct, overturned this: perfectionistic *concerns* (fear of mistakes, socially prescribed pressure) correlate positively with procrastination (r = 0.23, k = 43, N = 10,000), while perfectionistic *strivings* (self-oriented, achievement-focused) correlate negatively (r = −0.22, k = 38, N = 9,544) [peer-reviewed]. A separate meta-analysis (Xie et al.) found self-efficacy mediates the self-oriented-perfectionism-to-procrastination pathway [peer-reviewed]. **Contested:** whether "perfectionism causes procrastination" depends entirely on which subtype is measured — this is a genuine construct-splitting resolution, not merely conflicting findings.[^11][^12][^13]

**Rising-perfectionism cohort data.** Curran and Hill's landmark cross-temporal meta-analysis (164 samples, 41,641 college students, 1989–2016) found self-oriented, socially prescribed, and other-oriented perfectionism all increased linearly, coinciding with rising "neoliberal…competitive individualism" [peer-reviewed]. A follow-up study extending to 2024 (307 samples, ~83,000 students) found socially prescribed perfectionism accelerated on a quadratic (not linear) trajectory starting in the early 2000s, and that declining GDP per capita and rising inequality were associated with steeper increases in perfectionistic strivings and concerns respectively — while perfectionism-psychopathology correlations remained stable [peer-reviewed].[^14][^15][^16]

**Founder prevalence.** [SOURCE MISSING] for founder-specific perfectionism prevalence; the cohort data above is drawn from college-student samples and generalizes only inferentially to entrepreneurs.

**Interventions.** Because strivings and concerns pull in opposite directions, coaching should preserve self-oriented striving (linked to conscientiousness, organization) while targeting socially prescribed concerns (fear of others' judgment) [peer-reviewed]. Self-efficacy-building interventions are indicated given the mediation finding [peer-reviewed].[^13][^10]

**Founder language markers.** "I can't ship this until it's right," "What will investors think if this looks amateur," "I redid the deck for the fifth time."

## 3. Impostor Phenomenon

**Construct and research base.** Clance and Imes (1978) coined "impostor phenomenon" (not "syndrome" — they were explicit it is not a clinical disorder) from qualitative work with 150+ high-achieving women, describing persistent belief in one's own fraudulence despite objective success, with an "Impostor Cycle": pre-task anxiety leads to over-preparation or procrastination, success gets discounted, and self-doubt is reinforced rather than resolved [peer-reviewed].[^17][^18]

**Prevalence.** Bravata et al.'s 2020 systematic review of 62 studies (14,161 individuals) found prevalence estimates ranging from 9% to 82%, a spread the authors and later commentators attribute to inconsistent scales (CIPS vs. Harvey), cutoffs, and populations — meaning any single-number prevalence claim should be treated skeptically [peer-reviewed]. **Founder-specific data:** a 2024 Academy of Management study (28 qualitative interviews + a 226-entrepreneur Prolific sample) found 77% of surveyed entrepreneurs experienced rare-to-intense impostor experiences, which negatively predicted venture performance; the effect was mitigated by entrepreneurial network support [peer-reviewed]. Qualitative work also shows impostorism shapes founders' hiring decisions and their perception of venture scalability, generally in avoidant directions [peer-reviewed].[^19][^20][^21][^22]

**What actually helps.** Network/peer support measurably reduced the negative venture-performance association in the AOM study [peer-reviewed]. A quasi-experimental group-coaching trial among early-career software engineers found only modest reductions in impostor scores, with the control group improving comparably — suggesting impostor experiences respond more to contextual and team-level factors (feedback culture, project complexity) than to structured coaching alone [peer-reviewed]. This is an important caution against overselling coaching efficacy for this construct.[^23][^21]

**Founder language markers.** "I got lucky with this round," "Any day now someone's going to realize I don't know what I'm doing," "I don't deserve to be in this room."

## 4. Fear of Failure and Fear of Success

**Construct and research base.** Conroy's Performance Failure Appraisal Inventory (PFAI, 2001/2002) operationalizes fear of failure across five threat appraisals: fear of shame/embarrassment, fear of devaluing one's self-estimate, fear of an uncertain future, fear of important others losing interest, and fear of upsetting important others — these intercorrelate strongly enough to yield one higher-order "general fear of failure" score [peer-reviewed]. Fear of *success* is far less rigorously operationalized in peer-reviewed literature; most coverage is practitioner-level, often folded into self-handicapping research. [practitioner/contested][^24][^25]

**Self-handicapping tradition.** Berglas and Jones (1978) showed experimentally that after *noncontingent* success (success the person can't confidently attribute to their own ability), male participants preferentially chose performance-*inhibiting* drugs before a second test — protecting the ability attribution by manufacturing an alternative excuse for possible failure [peer-reviewed]. The mechanism relies on discounting (a handicap explains failure) and augmenting (success "despite" a handicap looks more impressive) [peer-reviewed]. Self-handicapping reliably *impairs* actual subsequent performance even as it protects self-image [peer-reviewed].[^26][^27][^28][^29]

**Founder prevalence.** [SOURCE MISSING] for direct PFAI norms in founder samples; qualitative entrepreneurship-challenge surveys list "fear of failure and too high expectations" among commonly self-reported personal challenges but without validated PFAI scoring [practitioner].[^30]

**Interventions.** Coaching can use PFAI's five-dimension structure diagnostically (ask which specific fear dominates — shame, self-devaluation, others' disappointment) rather than treating "fear of failure" as monolithic [peer-reviewed]. For self-handicapping, the coaching lever is making the ability-attribution stakes explicit and lowering them (e.g., normalizing that a launch is a test of the *market*, not a referendum on the founder's worth) — this is inferred from the discounting/augmenting mechanism rather than a direct RCT on entrepreneurs. [practitioner][^25]

**Founder language markers.** "I didn't really prepare for that pitch" (pre-emptive excuse), "If this succeeds, everyone will expect more from me," "I'm not sure I even want it to work."

## 5. Executive Function and ADHD in Entrepreneurs

**Construct and research base.** Johan Wiklund's research program, sparked by his own ADHD diagnosis, is the dominant line here. Wiklund, Yu, Tucker, and Marino (2017, *Journal of Business Venturing*) found inattention negatively but hyperactivity/impulsivity positively associated with entrepreneurship, with sensation-seeking and low premeditation as positive predictors and urgency (acting rashly under negative affect) as a negative one [peer-reviewed]. A large-scale study (Lerner, Verheul, Thurik; N = 9,869) found clinically diagnosed ADHD increased odds of entrepreneurial intention by ~1.7x and odds of actual venturing by ~100%, comparable in magnitude to having self-employed parents [peer-reviewed]. Wiklund, Patzelt, and Dimov's qualitative case study of 14 diagnosed entrepreneurs identified impulsivity as a driver of entrepreneurial action and hyperfocus as a double-edged catalyst — sometimes an asset, sometimes destructive [peer-reviewed].[^31][^32][^33]

**The "advantages" claim vs. its critique.** Yu, Wiklund, and Pérez-Luño (2021) found hyperactive/impulsive symptoms positively predict firm performance via entrepreneurial orientation, but inattention symptoms do not [peer-reviewed]. **Contested/critique:** A 2026 meta-analysis by Tran, Wiklund, Antshel, Jhawar, and Montgomery (298 effect sizes, 47 studies) — notably co-authored by Wiklund himself, functioning as a self-correction — found hyperactivity/impulsivity predicts entrepreneurial *attitudes and behaviors* but not *post-launch outcomes*; inattention is negatively associated with post-launch outcomes; and total ADHD symptoms/diagnosis, while positively linked to entrepreneurial behavior, are *negatively* linked to outcomes overall [peer-reviewed]. This directly complicates the "ADHD advantage" narrative: ADHD may push people *into* entrepreneurship without improving their odds of *succeeding* at it. A related Gestalt-perspective study found ADHD-symptom entrepreneurs only achieve strong performance when they combine passion for founding and developing while lacking excess passion for the ideation phase — i.e., the advantage is conditional, not automatic [peer-reviewed].[^34][^35][^36]

**Practical EF scaffolding.** ADHD in adults is linked to prospective-memory impairment and to task aversiveness/impulsivity mediating procrastination, with expectancy and impulsiveness as partial mediators [peer-reviewed]. Coaching-adaptable scaffolds (externalizing memory via checklists, reducing task-initiation friction, structuring accountability check-ins) are widely recommended in practitioner literature but lack founder-specific RCT evidence. [practitioner][^37]

**Founder language markers.** "I have twelve tabs open and can't remember what I was doing," "I get incredibly focused on the fun parts and avoid the boring admin entirely," "I keep starting new projects before finishing the last one."

## 6. Rumination and Worry

**Construct and research base.** Nolen-Hoeksema's Response Styles Theory (1991) holds that ruminative responses to negative mood — repetitively focusing on symptoms and their causes/consequences — prolong depressive episodes by biasing thinking negatively and interfering with problem-solving and instrumental behavior, whereas active distraction shortens them [peer-reviewed]. Nolen-Hoeksema and Wisco's 2008 review refined this: rumination more consistently predicts the *onset* of depression than its duration, and rumination interacts with negative cognitive styles to affect duration; contrary to original predictions, positive distraction hasn't consistently reduced depressive symptoms in correlational studies (though it does in experimental studies) [peer-reviewed]. Rumination is now linked to anxiety, binge eating, binge drinking, and self-harm beyond depression [peer-reviewed].[^38][^39]

**Founder prevalence.** [SOURCE MISSING] — no founder-specific rumination prevalence data surfaced in this search.

**Worry postponement/stimulus control.** Borkovec, Wilkinson, Folensbee, and Lerman's stimulus-control technique (1983) — scheduling a fixed daily worry window and deferring intrusive worries to it rather than suppressing them — is one of the best-supported CBT techniques for chronic worry and generalized anxiety [peer-reviewed]. It works by training the brain that worry doesn't require an immediate response, and many worries resolve or lose urgency by the scheduled time [practitioner-summarized peer-reviewed technique].[^40]

**Founder language markers.** "I keep replaying that investor meeting in my head," "I can't stop running worst-case scenarios about cash flow," "3am and I'm still thinking about the churn numbers."

## 7. Rejection Sensitivity and Avoidance

**Construct and research base.** Rejection sensitivity is well-studied in relational/social psychology (Downey & Feldman); applied to sales contexts, it manifests as call reluctance — a well-documented phenomenon (Dudley & Goodson's "Psychology of Sales Call Reluctance") distinguishing emotional hesitation (reluctance) from the behavioral outcome (avoidance) [practitioner]. Neuroimaging work cited in the sales literature associates lower rejection sensitivity with greater lateral prefrontal cortex activity during rejection processing, and social rejection is noted to activate neural pathways overlapping with physical pain [practitioner, citing peer-reviewed neuroscience]. This mechanism is best described as *experiential avoidance*: not dialing removes short-term discomfort, reinforcing avoidance over time [practitioner].[^41][^42]

**Graded exposure outside clinical settings.** Progressive/graduated exposure — starting with low-stakes calls (warm leads, existing customers, mock calls with colleagues) and escalating to cold prospects — mirrors clinical exposure-hierarchy principles and is the most consistently recommended non-clinical intervention in the sales-coaching literature [practitioner]. This is an analogical extension of exposure therapy rather than a direct RCT on founders. [practitioner, principle borrowed from peer-reviewed clinical exposure literature][^42][^43]

**Founder prevalence.** [SOURCE MISSING] for founder-specific rejection-sensitivity data; most evidence here is sales-practitioner literature, not peer-reviewed entrepreneurship research.

**Founder language markers.** "I keep putting off that follow-up call," "I'd rather build more features than actually ask for the sale," "Every 'no' feels like it's about me."

## 8. Decision Fatigue and Choice Overload

**Construct and current replication status — contested.** Decision fatigue is conventionally explained via "ego depletion" (Baumeister), the idea that self-control draws on a limited resource. This foundational theory has largely **failed to replicate**: a preregistered 23-laboratory Registered Replication Report (Hagger, Chatzisarantis et al., 2016; N = 2,141) using a standardized depletion manipulation and outcome measure found a pooled effect of d ≈ 0.04 (95% CI −0.06 to 0.13) — indistinguishable from zero [peer-reviewed]. A second multisite preregistered test designed with input from the theory's own proponents (Vohs, Schmeichel et al., 2021, *Psychological Science*) again found effects too small to support the standard resource account [peer-reviewed]. Bias-corrected meta-analyses (Carter, Kofler, Forster, McCullough, 2015) concluded that once publication bias is corrected for, little evidence remains for a depletion effect of consequential size [peer-reviewed].[^44][^45]

Choice overload (the "jam study" popularization: more options reduce satisfaction/choice) shows a parallel replication problem: Scheibehenne, Greifeneder, and Todd's 2010 meta-analysis (*Journal of Consumer Research*) found a mean effect size close to zero across pooled experiments, with high heterogeneity — sometimes more options help, sometimes they hurt, and the moderators are not well established [peer-reviewed]. A more recent econometric critique argues existing tests are statistically underpowered and that choice overload may be more prevalent than the null meta-analytic result suggests once measurement is improved [peer-reviewed, contested]. **Verdict: contested — both "decision fatigue is real and large" and "decision fatigue is a myth" overstate the evidence; the honest position is that the original resource-depletion mechanism is not supported, though something context-dependent about repeated choice-making likely remains unexplained.** A 2026 review proposes replacing the resource metaphor with a "Choice Strain Model" combining cognitive load, motivational cost-benefit evaluation, and affective aversion [peer-reviewed, novel/unreplicated].[^46][^47][^45][^44]

**Founder-specific evidence.** [SOURCE MISSING] — no founder-specific decision-fatigue studies surfaced.

**Coaching implication.** Given the contested evidence, an AI mentor should avoid confidently invoking "your willpower is depleted" framing. What likely *does* transfer practically (independent of the depletion mechanism) is reducing the *number* and *complexity* of decisions a founder must make in a day via defaults, delegation, and pre-decision (not because willpower runs out, but because unclear decision criteria and high perceived stakes increase decision-avoidance). [practitioner, mechanism-agnostic]

**Founder language markers.** "I can't decide what to have for lunch after a day of vendor negotiations," "By 4pm I just start saying yes to everything to make it stop," "I keep re-deciding things I already decided."

## 9. Self-Criticism vs. Self-Compassion

**Construct and research base.** Kristin Neff's model (2003) defines self-compassion as three components: self-kindness (vs. self-judgment), common humanity (vs. isolation), and mindfulness (vs. over-identification with pain) [peer-reviewed]. MacBeth and Gumley's 2012 meta-analysis (20 studies, ~4,000+ participants) found a large inverse effect between self-compassion and psychopathology (r ≈ 0.54) [peer-reviewed]. A well-being meta-analysis found r ≈ 0.47 between self-compassion and well-being (k = 79, N = 16,416), with a causal subsample confirming direction: manipulated state self-compassion improved affective well-being (Hedges' g up to 0.90) and trait interventions improved overall well-being (g = 0.36) [peer-reviewed].[^48][^49]

**Does self-compassion undermine motivation? (The key contested question, resolved by data.)** The intuitive fear — that being kind to yourself after failure removes the "edge" needed to improve — is **not supported**. Breines and Chen's four-experiment study found participants induced into self-compassion after personal failure showed *more* motivation to make amends, study harder, and learn from mistakes than those in self-esteem-boost or distraction conditions [peer-reviewed]. Neff (2005) found self-compassion positively predicts *mastery* goals (intrinsic motivation to learn/grow) and negatively predicts *performance* goals (extrinsic desire for social approval) — meaning self-compassionate people remain achievement-motivated, just for different reasons [peer-reviewed]. A 2021 meta-analysis of 60 studies (cited in Neff's 2023 review) found a medium positive association between self-compassion and self-efficacy [peer-reviewed].[^50][^51][^49]

**Self-compassion vs. self-esteem.** Neff and Vonk (2009) found self-compassion produces more *stable* self-worth (less contingent on outcomes/comparison) than self-esteem, which fluctuates with performance and social comparison [peer-reviewed]. A 2023 narrative review/meta-analysis found that after controlling for shared variance, both self-compassion and self-esteem retain unique, comparably-sized independent contributions to well-being — i.e., neither fully subsumes the other [peer-reviewed].[^52][^49]

**Interventions.** A meta-analysis of self-compassion-related interventions specifically for reducing self-criticism (20 RCTs, 1,350 participants) found a medium effect, Hedges' g = 0.51, 95% CI [0.33, 0.69], with larger effects for longer interventions compared against passive (not active) controls [peer-reviewed]. A broader RCT meta-analysis of self-compassion interventions on psychosocial outcomes (Ferrari et al., 2019) supports general efficacy [peer-reviewed].[^53][^54][^55]

**Founder language markers.** "I should be further along by now," "I can't believe I made that mistake in the investor call — what an idiot," "I don't have time to be kind to myself, I need to fix this."

## 10. Limiting Beliefs / Mindset (Dweck)

**Construct and research base.** Carol Dweck's mindset theory posits that a "growth mindset" (belief that abilities are malleable) versus a "fixed mindset" (belief abilities are innate/static) predicts resilience to failure and achievement [peer-reviewed, original theory]. This is one of the most popularized — and most meta-analytically scrutinized — constructs in this report.

**Meta-analytic critique (the central contested finding).** Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler, and Macnamara (2018, *Psychological Science*) ran two meta-analyses: (1) 273 correlational effect sizes (~365,000 students) found mindset-achievement correlation r ≈ 0.10 — small, accounting for roughly 1% of achievement variance; (2) 43 intervention studies (~57,000 students) found average causal effect of mindset interventions on achievement d ≈ 0.08 — a small effect meaningfully larger only for academically high-risk or low-SES subgroups, with substantial evidence of publication bias in the underlying literature [peer-reviewed]. Macnamara and Burgoyne's 2023 update (*Psychological Bulletin*) reinforced this: aggregate effects remain d ≈ 0.05–0.10, publication bias is substantial, and after bias correction and restricting to studies where the mindset manipulation actually changed beliefs, effects on achievement became statistically non-significant (d̄ = 0.02–0.04) [peer-reviewed]. This is a critical distinction: interventions reliably change *stated beliefs* about intelligence but this belief change does not reliably translate into changed *achievement outcomes* [peer-reviewed].[^56][^57][^58][^59]

**What survives.** Yeager, Hanselman, Walton et al.'s large 2019 national RCT (*Nature*, 12,490 U.S. ninth-graders, two 25-minute online interventions) found a small but statistically robust GPA improvement (~0.10 points) specifically among lower-achieving students, and crucially, **only when the school's normative environment supported challenge-taking** — the effect did not appear in unsupportive school climates [peer-reviewed]. This is the most credible surviving finding: growth-mindset effects are real, small, concentrated in struggling/high-risk populations, and *conditional on a supportive environment*, not a universal performance lever [peer-reviewed].[^60][^59][^56]

**Founder-specific evidence.** [SOURCE MISSING] — no entrepreneur-specific growth-mindset RCTs surfaced; the environment-contingency finding (works only where surrounding social norms support risk-taking) is plausibly relevant to startup culture but this is inference, not direct evidence.

**Coaching implication.** An AI mentor should not present "just adopt a growth mindset" as a high-leverage lever — the honest evidence-based framing is that mindset language may help modestly for founders who are struggling and operating within a genuinely supportive peer/investor environment, and is unlikely to move the needle for founders who are already high-functioning or who operate in unsupportive, high-judgment ecosystems [peer-reviewed, synthesized].[^59][^60]

**Founder language markers.** "I've just never been a numbers person," "Some people are natural salespeople and I'm not one of them," "I'm not the risk-taking type."

## Interactions Between Blockers

These constructs rarely operate in isolation; the most clinically and practically important pattern is a cascading spiral:

**Perfectionism → procrastination → shame spiral.** Socially prescribed perfectionistic *concerns* (fear of others' judgment) drive avoidance of starting a task that might be judged imperfect (procrastination, r = 0.23) [peer-reviewed]. The delay itself generates guilt and shame (Sirois & Pychyl's mood-repair model), which becomes new negative affect requiring its own repair — often via further avoidance or rumination — reinforcing the cycle [peer-reviewed]. Self-criticism (the opposite of self-compassion) intensifies the shame phase, and Breines & Chen's findings suggest this actually *reduces* the founder's ability to recover motivation, compared to a self-compassionate response [peer-reviewed].[^2][^5][^12][^49]

**Impostor phenomenon → self-handicapping → avoidant hiring/scaling decisions.** The AOM entrepreneur study found impostor experiences shape hiring choices and scalability perceptions in generally avoidant directions [peer-reviewed] — this maps onto the self-handicapping mechanism (Berglas & Jones): a founder who doubts their competence may under-invest in growth infrastructure so that failure can be attributed to "we never really tried to scale" rather than "I'm not capable of running a bigger company" [peer-reviewed, inferred connection].[^21][^26]

**Fear of failure ↔ fear of success ↔ rejection sensitivity in sales.** A founder with high fear of shame/embarrassment (Conroy's FSE dimension) [peer-reviewed] often manifests identically to high rejection sensitivity in sales contexts — both produce call/ask avoidance — making differential diagnosis important (see table below) [practitioner].[^61][^25]

**ADHD/executive-function deficits → decision fatigue-like exhaustion → rumination at night.** Inattention symptoms are linked to worse post-launch outcomes [peer-reviewed] partly because unmanaged task-switching and prospective-memory failures [peer-reviewed] create more unresolved open loops, which plausibly feed nighttime rumination and worry, even though the "resource depletion" mechanism itself is contested [peer-reviewed].[^36][^37][^44]

## The Referral Boundary: What Is Beyond Coaching

A non-clinical AI mentor operates on the assumption of *sub-clinical* variation in normal founder psychology. Certain presentations exceed this scope and require licensed-professional referral:

- **Persistent depressive symptoms** (Nolen-Hoeksema's rumination research is explicitly about depression, not just "founder stress") — anhedonia, hopelessness, or rumination lasting weeks with functional impairment beyond work [peer-reviewed basis for concern].[^39][^38]
- **Clinically diagnosable ADHD** — Wiklund's own research is explicit that his subjects carry formal ADHD *diagnoses*; coaching can scaffold executive function but cannot diagnose or treat ADHD, and inattention symptoms are linked to *worse* outcomes without appropriate treatment support [peer-reviewed].[^32][^33]
- **Panic-level anxiety or clinical generalized anxiety disorder** — worry-postponement is a CBT technique typically used *within* or alongside professional treatment for GAD, not as a standalone substitute [peer-reviewed].[^40]
- **Self-handicapping via substance use** — the original Berglas and Jones paradigm specifically identified drug/alcohol choice as a self-handicapping strategy; any founder describing this pattern needs referral, not coaching [peer-reviewed].[^27][^26]
- **Impostor-phenomenon-linked suicidal ideation or severe burnout with health impact** — impostor phenomenon itself is explicitly non-clinical (Clance and Imes were emphatic about this), but its downstream effects can reach clinical severity, especially compounded with rejection sensitivity and shame spirals [peer-reviewed].[^17]
- **Trauma-linked rejection sensitivity** — if avoidance patterns trace to identifiable past trauma rather than general performance anxiety, graded exposure without clinical oversight risks harm; this is the domain of licensed exposure therapy, not sales coaching [peer-reviewed, by extension of clinical exposure literature].[^62]

The operating rule for an AI mentor: coach the *pattern* (procrastination, perfectionism, decision friction, limiting self-talk) using the interventions graded above, and flag for referral whenever the founder's language indicates functional impairment beyond the business (sleep, relationships, physical health, safety) rather than performance friction within it.
